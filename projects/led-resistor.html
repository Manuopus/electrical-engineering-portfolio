LED with a current-limiting resistor

Calculate the resistor that keeps a red LED at a safe current on a 5 V supply, then build the circuit and compare the measured current with the calculation.

Objective

An LED is not a resistor: once the voltage across it reaches its forward voltage, its current rises very steeply. Connected straight to 5 V it would draw far too much current and burn out. The goal is to choose a series resistor that limits the current to a safe value, and to prove the choice by measurement.

Theory

Ohm's law

For a resistor, voltage U (volts, V), resistance R (ohms, Ω) and current I (amperes, A) are linked by:

U = R · I   ⇔   R = U / I   ⇔   I = U / R

Kirchhoff's voltage law in this circuit

In a series loop, the supply voltage is shared between the parts. The LED takes its forward voltage UF, and the resistor takes the rest:

UB = UF + UR   ⇒   UR = UB − UF

Because the parts are in series, the same current flows through the resistor and the LED. So setting the resistor's current sets the LED's current.

Design and calculation

Given values

  • Supply voltage: UB = 5.0 V
  • LED forward voltage (typical red LED): UF ≈ 2.0 V
  • Target current: I = 15 mA = 0.015 A (below the usual 20 mA maximum)

Step 1: voltage across the resistor

UR = UB − UF

UR = 5.0 V − 2.0 V

UR = 3.0 V

Step 2: required resistance

R = UR / I

R = 3.0 V / 0.015 A

R = 200 Ω

200 Ω is not a standard value. The next higher E12 value is 220 Ω. Rounding up keeps the current slightly lower, which is the safe side.

Step 3: actual current with 220 Ω

I = UR / R

I = 3.0 V / 220 Ω

I ≈ 0.0136 A = 13.6 mA

Step 4: power in the resistor

P = UR · I

P = 3.0 V · 0.0136 A

P ≈ 0.041 W = 41 mW

A ¼ W resistor is rated for 250 mW, about six times more than needed, so it stays cool.
